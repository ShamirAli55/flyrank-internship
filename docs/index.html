Search Intelligence: Predicting Content Performance Decline

Author: Shamir Ali

Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Project: FlyRank ML Internship

Date: August 2026

Abstract

Can historical search-performance metrics identify content pages at risk of an impressions decline in the following month? Using the FlyRank internship warehouse (85,475 active content pages, Feb–Mar 2026), we trained a Random Forest classifier on five query-level features and evaluated it on a client-grouped held-out split. After correcting for temporal leakage found in the Week-6 audit, the audited model achieved 38.1% precision on the decline class versus a 9.6% baseline precision, though recall remained modest at 6.8%. The output is a ranked priority queue intended for editorial decision-support — not a causal forecast.

1. Introduction

Content teams must regularly decide which pages to review and refresh. Without tooling, the choice is either arbitrary or relies on manual spot-checks. The goal of this project is to rank pages by their estimated risk of an impressions drop, so editors can focus limited review time on the pages that need it most.

A wrong recommendation wastes editorial effort (false positive) or lets a declining page go unnoticed (false negative). This system is therefore treated as a decision-support prioritization tool — a human editor makes the final call.

2. Data

Source: FlyRank/internship-warehouse on Hugging Face.

Tables used: fact_content_daily_performance and fact_content_query_90d.

  • Feature window: February 2026 impressions and query signals.
  • Outcome window: March 2026 impressions (used only to construct the binary label).
  • Scope: 85,475 content pages with positive February impressions after null-dropping.

3. Methodology

Target

A page is labelled declining (1) if its March 2026 impressions are less than 80% of its February 2026 impressions — i.e., a drop of more than 20%. All other pages are labelled 0. The decline class makes up approximately 14.3% of the dataset.

Baseline (Week-4)

A simple visibility-based rule: pages with ≥ 1,000 prior impressions and an average GSC position between 4 and 15 are flagged as high-risk. This is a fair representation of common editorial intuition.

Model (Week-5 → Audited in Week-6)

A Random Forest classifier (200 estimators, class_weight="balanced", random_state=42). Balanced class weights compensate for the imbalanced target.

Validation

Data split using GroupShuffleSplit with client_hash_id as the group key (80/20, random_state=42). This ensures no client seen in training appears in testing, giving a realistic measure of generalization to new domains.

  • Train: 54,384 rows, 29 clients
  • Test: 31,091 rows, 8 clients

The Week-6 audit confirmed that the 90-day query aggregate statistics are historical aggregates and do not embed future outcome information, so they remain valid features after the audit.

4. Results

Evaluated on the held-out client-grouped test set (31,091 pages, 14.3% decline base rate):

Accuracy0.8710.668
Precision0.3810.096
Recall0.0680.201
F1 Score0.1160.130

The model is more precise than the baseline (38.1% vs 9.6%), meaning fewer false alarms per flagged page. However, it catches a smaller share of actually declining pages (recall 6.8% vs 20.1%). The trade-off is: the model flags a smaller, higher-confidence set; the baseline flags more pages but most are false positives given the low base rate.

Key finding: After removing lookahead leakage in the Week-6 audit, the model's F1 dropped from ~0.74 (naive split) to 0.116. This confirms that the original inflated performance was an artefact of data leakage — not real predictive signal. Both the model and baseline have similar F1, suggesting this is a genuinely hard prediction task with the available features alone.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The model identifies statistical associations in the data — it does not explain why impressions decline.
  • It does not replicate or predict Google's ranking algorithm.
  • Seasonality, content quality, and indexing changes are not captured in the features.
  • Evaluated on a single two-month window (Feb–Mar 2026); performance on other periods is unknown.
  • A client-grouped split was used, but 8 test clients is a small sample — results are directional.
  • Every recommendation requires a human review before any editorial action is taken.

Results are best described as observed, directional decision-support evidence — not a production deployment or a causal claim.
